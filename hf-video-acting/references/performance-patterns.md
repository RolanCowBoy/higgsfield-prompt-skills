# Performance patterns

Informed by PASSPORT RUSH records 5, 9–10, 36, 49, 59 and 86. These are authored adaptations, not tested rendering recipes.

## Task-based performance
Directing brief: a person who is losing hope looks for evidence that a partner still has a plan.
Useful visible translation: inspect the obstacle, check the partner, receive a short signal, pause to reassess.
Keep the biography and private history in the directing brief unless they create visible choices.

Do not mechanically prohibit facial cues. “He wants her to trust him” needs observable staging; a small nod, glance, release of a grip, or smile can carry it.

## A short mischievous beat
Given an approved character and a soft toy, a possible four-second single-shot plan:
- start already holding the toy behind the back;
- look toward an offscreen adult, then briefly at the hiding hand;
- pull the toy a little farther out of sight and suppress a smile;
- end still holding it behind the back.

Use the actual object's affordances. A rigid toy cannot fold like cloth; a puppet worn on a hand cannot independently change hands without a transfer.
Keep the gesture visible enough for the audience while plausibly hidden from the partner. These are different sightlines.

## Broad expression and restrained intention
For an exaggerated comic surprise: a fast eye/head reaction, a held readable pose, then delayed hair or cloth settling may be appropriate.
For concealed suspicion: a small shift in attention and a purposeful pause may be sufficient.
Choose the user's register. Do not replace “grotesque cartoon” with restrained live-action behavior because the task-based method sounds more sophisticated.

## Mirror scene
A mirror may reveal one character at first and another after rotation. Place their visible reactions accordingly.
A driver looking into a mirror and a passenger looking toward that mirror do not automatically have mutual visible eye contact for the audience. Confirm geometry or use a simpler readable signal.
Avoid simultaneous commands to pin the eyes to the road, look at the partner, and never move the gaze. Assign phases with an intelligible priority.

## Timing and stillness
Treat “one frame of recognition” as an accent to test, not an assurance of exact frame scheduling.
If a shot is genuinely frozen, a held still image may be the appropriate production method. If the task is a living pause, preserve breathing, balance and small responsive movement.
Maintain environmental motion when the performance pauses in a moving vehicle.

## Acceptance checks
Can a viewer identify what the character is trying to do without reading the prompt? Is the decisive event visible? Does the emotional turn follow an event rather than random face changes? Do object ownership, body contacts and identity remain coherent? Does the final state match the next shot's intended start?
